Research

The question

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) solve a differential equation by training a network to make the equation's residual small at sampled points. They work well for smooth problems and struggle when the solution has steep gradients, high-frequency oscillation or stiffness: networks tend to fit low frequencies first, and the separate loss terms are hard to balance.

Quantum PINNs replace part of the network with a parameterized quantum circuit. Such circuits can represent rich functions with few trainable parameters, but on their own they run into the same difficulties with multiscale solutions, and differentiating through them is expensive in simulation.

My work asks what changes when the quantum model is paired with a basis suited to the problem, such as wavelets for localized multiscale features, and with formulations that handle nonlocal operators. I try to be as careful about the costs as about the gains: how many parameters and qubits a method needs, what it costs to simulate, and which conclusions a given experiment actually supports.

Wavelet bases in quantum PINNs

Wavelets are localized in both position and scale. A function with a sharp front needs many Fourier modes to describe, but only a handful of fine-scale wavelet coefficients near the front. The figure on the home page shows this on the benchmark solutions used below. The two papers in this line of work build that property into quantum PINNs.

arXiv preprint, December 2025

Wavelet-Accelerated Physics-Informed Quantum Neural Network for Multiscale Partial Differential Equations

Deepak Gupta, Himanshu Pandey, Ratikanta Behera

  • arXiv:2512.08256
  • PDF

The idea

Instead of asking a circuit for the value of the solution at each point, WPIQNN writes the solution as a finite wavelet expansion and lets the hybrid network supply the coefficients:

u^(x)=∑j,kcjk(θ)ψjk(x)+B, ψjk(x)=2j/2ψ(2jx−k), ψ(x)=−xe−x2/2,

over a chosen range of scales j, with tensor products of these functions in more than one dimension. Because the basis is fixed, its derivatives can be computed analytically once and stored as matrices. The PDE, boundary and initial residuals are then assembled from those matrices and the coefficients, without automatic differentiation through the network for the residual itself. Gradients with respect to the circuit parameters θ, needed for training, are still computed in the usual way.

Experiments

The paper uses four benchmark families with known exact solutions: a heat-conduction problem whose solution develops a sharp peak as a parameter ε decreases (from 0.5 down to 0.11), a Helmholtz equation in a high-frequency regime, a Klein–Gordon equation with fast oscillation in time, and one-dimensional Maxwell equations in homogeneous and heterogeneous media. The baselines are a classical PINN, two earlier quantum PINN designs (PIQNN-I and PIQNN-II) and a classical wavelet-based PINN (W-PINN). Errors are relative L2 errors averaged over ten runs.

What the paper reports

  • On every benchmark, WPIQNN's mean error is lower than that of the classical wavelet PINN, while using 1.3–1.8% of its trainable parameters.
  • For ε between 0.14 and 0.11, where the paper reports that the other methods did not converge, WPIQNN reaches errors of order 10⁻⁴.
  • In a timing study on the heat problem, training is about 1.8 to 2.3 times faster than PIQNN-I and 3.9 to 5.7 times faster than PIQNN-II, depending on circuit depth.

What it does not show

All experiments use a noiseless state-vector simulator, so the comparisons concern accuracy, parameter count and simulated training time. They say nothing about performance on quantum hardware and are not evidence of a quantum speedup. The timing comparison is against other quantum PINNs; the paper does not compare wall-clock time with the classical W-PINN, because simulating the circuits on classical hardware is itself costly.

Manuscript under review

ER-WPIQNN: Energy-Refined Wavelet-Based Quantum PINNs for PDEs with Sharp Localized Structures

WPIQNN fixes its wavelet dictionary in advance. For solutions with sharp, localized features a larger dictionary helps resolve them, but many of its wavelets end up carrying little information and add redundancy. ER-WPIQNN works in two stages. A short initial WPIQNN training stage gives a first estimate of the wavelet coefficients; an energy-based criterion then selects the most informative wavelets, and the model is refined classically using only those. The aim is a smaller, better-targeted basis for problems with localized structure.

Details and results will appear here once the manuscript is public.

Quantum PINNs for nonlocal equations

Models of viscoelasticity, anomalous diffusion and heat transfer with memory involve integrals over a solution's history or fractional derivatives. Such nonlocal operators do not fit the pointwise residual of a standard PINN and need their own treatment.

arXiv preprint, June 2026

Quantum Physics-Informed Neural Networks for Solving Integro and Fractional PDEs

Deepak Gupta, Ratikanta Behera

  • arXiv:2606.26865
  • PDF

Problem classes

The paper treats nonlinear integro-differential equations of the form

ℒ[u](x)=f(x)+λ∫𝒟(x)K(x,z)𝒩(u(z))dz,

coupled systems of such equations, and nonlinear time-fractional integro-PDEs that combine Caputo derivatives with fractional integrals, where

Dtαu(t)=1Γ(1−α)∫0t∂su(s)(t−s)−αds,0<α<1.

The model

Inputs pass through a trainable affine map z=Wx+b; each zi is encoded as a rotation Ry(zi) on its own qubit, followed by layers of single-qubit rotations and a ring of CNOTs. A linear readout of the Pauli-Z expectations gives the trial solution uθ(x)=𝐜⊤Φ(x)+c0. Each measured expectation is a trigonometric polynomial in the affine variables, and that structure leads to the approximation result: for a probability measure μ and a target f with integrable Fourier transform, an n-qubit circuit of this form can achieve

‖f−uθ‖L2(μ)≤‖f^‖L1n.

This is an existence statement, proved with a Monte Carlo sampling argument in the spirit of Barron-type bounds for neural networks. It says that suitable parameters exist, not that training will find them.

Two treatments of the nonlocal terms

N-QPINN
Evaluates integrals and fractional operators by quadrature (Gauss–Legendre and trapezoidal rules, and a graded time mesh for the Caputo derivative); local derivatives by automatic differentiation. Covers all three problem classes.
A-QPINN
Introduces auxiliary variables for the integral terms, turning each equation into an equivalent local system that a multi-output circuit solves jointly, with no quadrature. Not applicable to the fractional problem.

Experiments

Four problems with known solutions: a scalar nonlinear Volterra equation, a coupled Volterra system, a two-dimensional Volterra integro-PDE and a time-fractional integro-PDE. The classical baseline is an auxiliary-variable PINN (A-PINN). Errors are relative L2 errors averaged over five runs.

Limits

As with the wavelet work, everything runs on a noiseless simulator, and the problems are small. The approximation theorem is an existence result. A-QPINN cannot handle fractional operators, and the paper lists noise-aware training and hardware runs as future work.

Exploring: quantum neural operators

A PINN solves one instance of a problem. A neural operator learns the solution map itself, for example from an initial condition or a coefficient field to the solution, so that a new instance is solved by a single forward pass. The wavelet neural operator of Tripura and Chakraborty does this with layers that move into wavelet coefficients, act on them, and transform back.

That structure raises the question I am now working on: can a quantum wavelet transform take the place of the classical one inside such a layer, with trainable circuits acting on the coefficients? Before claiming anything I want to answer the practical questions: how data enters and leaves the circuit, how circuit depth and parameter count scale, and whether there is a regime in which a quantum layer is competitive once those costs are counted. My quantum wavelet transform implementation is one of the pieces this would need.

This direction has no published results yet.

How to read the numbers

All the results on this site come from noiseless state-vector simulation of quantum circuits on classical hardware, using PennyLane's default.qubit simulator with PyTorch. They show how accurately a circuit-based model of a given size can be trained on a problem and how its parameter count compares with classical models.

They do not show how the methods behave on quantum hardware, where noise and finite measurement samples matter, and they are not evidence of quantum advantage. Timing comparisons are between simulated models. Loading data into a quantum state and reading results out of it have costs that simulation hides, and any honest comparison with classical solvers has to count them.
