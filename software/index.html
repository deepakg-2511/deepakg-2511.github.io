Why wavelets

The quantum Fourier transform is a standard subroutine, available in every major quantum software library. Many problems in signal processing and numerical analysis, including the PDEs in my own research, are better described by wavelets, which are localized in both position and scale. Quantum wavelet transforms have been studied since the late 1990s, starting with Fijany and Williams, and the multilevel two-dimensional version of Li et al. extends them to images and fields. I wanted a gate-level implementation of it that I could inspect, test and reuse as a building block.

How the circuit works

A 2n×2m array is amplitude-encoded on n+m qubits: the first register indexes the row and the second the column, so entry (r,c) becomes the amplitude of the basis state |r⟩|c⟩.

One level of the Haar transform on a register is a Hadamard gate on its last qubit, which forms normalized sums and differences of neighbouring entries, followed by a perfect-shuffle permutation that moves that qubit to the front so the sums and differences collect in two halves:

W2nH=P2n−1,2(I2n−1⊗H).

The perfect shuffle is a cyclic rotation of the qubits and is built from a chain of SWAP gates. Applying this to both registers gives the 2D transform, and the inverse runs the same gates in reverse. The Daubechies-4 version replaces the Hadamard step with the operator D2np of Li et al., built from a triangular network of multi-controlled X gates and two fixed single-qubit rotations. Further levels apply the transform again to the remaining qubits of each register.

What it computes

What is implemented

  • Amplitude encoding of grayscale images and RGB arrays whose sides are powers of two.
  • One-dimensional Haar and Daubechies-4 transforms, with their inverses.
  • Two-dimensional transforms acting on the row and column registers.
  • Multilevel forward and inverse transforms for both wavelets, in one and two dimensions.
  • Circuits written with elementary gates (Hadamard, SWAP, multi-controlled X with zero-controls, single-qubit unitaries) rather than dense matrices.

How it was checked

The notebook in the repository checks that the encoding reproduces the normalized input, and that each forward transform followed by its inverse returns the original state: for 1D and 2D Haar and Daubechies-4 transforms, and for multilevel transforms of a 512 × 512 image, which is an 18-qubit simulation.

Scope and cost

The number of gates in the transform grows only polynomially with the number of qubits, but that is not a speedup over the classical wavelet transform. Preparing the encoded state from classical data and reading out the coefficients generally cost at least as much as computing the transform classically. A quantum wavelet transform is useful as a subroutine inside algorithms whose data already lives in a quantum state, and that is how I am interested in using it: as a multiscale feature map for quantum models, and as a candidate component for the quantum neural operators I am exploring.

An earlier, work-in-progress experiment in packaging quantum wavelet transforms as reusable PennyLane operations. It defines Haar and Daubechies-4 transforms and the perfect-shuffle and bit-reversal permutations as Operation classes with gate decompositions, resource counts and unit tests, together with notes on the theory. It follows A. Fijany and C. P. Williams, “Quantum wavelet transforms: fast algorithms and complete circuits” (1998).
